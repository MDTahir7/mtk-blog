Do you remember how the world felt when you were a child?

A rainy day was not simply “bad weather.” It was an event.  
A puddle on the road could become a small ocean. A cardboard box could become a house, a car, or a spaceship. The sound of thunder could make you run to the window. Even the moon seemed strangely close, as if it were following you wherever you went.

Then, somehow, we grew up.  
The same rain became an inconvenience. The same moon became something we barely notice. The sounds around us became background noise. Places we once found fascinating became ordinary.

What happened?

Perhaps the world did not become less interesting.  
Perhaps we became better at ignoring it.

## The Filter We Develop

When we are children, our minds are constantly being introduced to new things. Almost everything deserves attention because almost everything is unfamiliar.  
As we grow older, this changes.  
Our brain cannot possibly give equal attention to everything around us. Imagine trying to consciously notice every sound, every face, every movement, every smell, every color, every conversation and every object around you.

It would be exhausting.

So the adult mind becomes selective.  
It learns what matters and what doesn't.  
The sound of the fan disappears into the background. The traffic outside becomes something we barely hear. We stop looking at the buildings we pass every day. We know the route to work so well that sometimes we arrive without remembering much of the journey. This ability is useful.  
It allows us to function. But there is a hidden cost. The same filter that protects us from information overload can also protect us from wonder.

## When Familiarity Kills Curiosity

Think about the first time you saw the ocean. You probably looked at it differently than you would today.  
The enormous amount of water, the sound of the waves, the horizon disappearing into the distance—it could feel almost impossible to comprehend.

But after seeing it enough times, the brain begins to categorize it.

“Ocean.”

Done.

The mystery is reduced to a familiar word.  
This happens everywhere.  
A person you have known for years becomes “my friend.”  
Your neighborhood becomes “home.”  
The sunrise becomes “morning.”  
The sky becomes something that is simply there.

We don't necessarily stop seeing these things.  
We stop looking at them.  
And there is a difference.

## Childhood Was Not Better—It Was Different

This doesn't mean childhood was some perfect period that we need to return to.  
Children can be impatient, restless, overwhelmed and easily distracted. Their minds are still developing, and the enormous amount of information around them can sometimes be difficult to handle.  
But there was something they possessed that many adults slowly lose:  
the ability to be genuinely fascinated by ordinary things.

A child doesn't need a luxury vacation to experience wonder.  
Give a child a magnifying glass and a few ants.  
Give them a flashlight in a dark room.  
Give them a box.  
Give them a puddle after the rain.  
Suddenly, there is a world to explore.

Adults often need something extraordinary before they allow themselves to feel amazed.  
Perhaps that is one of the great tragedies of growing up.

We keep searching for extraordinary experiences while ignoring the extraordinary things that are already around us.

## What If We Opened the Aperture Again?

There is an interesting possibility here.  
What if growing up doesn't require us to completely lose that childhood way of seeing?

What if we can deliberately reopen our attention?

Not permanently.  
Not by becoming childish.  
Just for a few minutes.

The next time you walk outside, try something simple.  
Don't listen to music.  
Don't check your phone.  
Don't think about your next task.

Just look.

Look at the buildings.  
Notice the different shades of color.  
Listen to the sounds around you.  
Watch how people move.  
Notice the wind.  
Look at the sky.

Pay attention to something you normally ignore.

At first, it may feel pointless.  
Then something strange can happen.  
You begin noticing things that were always there.  
The world hasn't changed.  
Your attention has changed.

## The Forgotten Art of Paying Attention

Perhaps wonder isn't something we lose completely.  
Perhaps it is something we stop giving our attention to.  
We live in a time when our attention is constantly being pulled in different directions.

Notifications.  
Messages.  
Videos.  
News.  
Social media.  
Work.  
Deadlines.

Our minds are trained to jump from one thing to another.  
And then we wonder why the world feels dull.  
Maybe the problem isn't that there is nothing interesting left.  
Maybe we have forgotten how to sit with something long enough for it to become interesting.

A tree can be boring if you glance at it for one second.  
But observe it for five minutes and you begin to notice the movement of its leaves, the patterns in its branches, the light passing through them, the insects moving around it.

The object hasn't changed.  
Your level of attention has.

## Maybe Growing Up Doesn't Mean Seeing Less

Growing up gives us many valuable things.  
Experience.  
Knowledge.  
Judgment.  
Discipline.  
The ability to distinguish what matters from what doesn't. We shouldn't throw those things away. But perhaps adulthood should not mean permanently closing the door to curiosity.

We can have an adult mind and still maintain a child's sense of wonder.  
We can understand how something works and still be amazed that it exists.  
We can know that the moon is a giant rock orbiting Earth and still look at it and think:  
How strange and beautiful that is.  
We can understand that rain is simply water falling from the atmosphere and still enjoy standing near a window listening to it.

Knowledge doesn't have to destroy wonder.  
Sometimes, it can deepen it.

## Seeing the World Again

Maybe the goal isn't to become a child again.  
We can't—and shouldn't—go backward.  
The goal is something more interesting:  
To become an adult who can still see the world with fresh eyes.  
To occasionally put down the phone.  
To walk somewhere without headphones.  
To look at something familiar as if you've never seen it before.  
To allow yourself to be curious about ordinary things.  
To notice what your brain has learned to ignore.

Because perhaps the world we experienced as children is still here.

The colors are still here.  
The sounds are still here.  
The stars are still there.  
The rain is still falling.

The only difference is that somewhere along the way, we built a filter between ourselves and the world.  
And maybe, just for a moment, we can open it again.

Not to escape adulthood.  
But to remember what it felt like to be fully present in the world.
